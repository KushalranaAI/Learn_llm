Imagine judging restaurants by how many times reviewers write "delicious". The first mention tells you a lot. The fifth tells you a little more. The fiftieth, in a single rambling review, tells you mostly that the reviewer is long-winded. **BM25** (short for "Best Match 25", the 25th variant its authors tried) builds exactly those two intuitions into the TF-IDF formula: repeated mentions should give diminishing returns, and long documents should not win just by being long. It has been the default keyword ranking function for decades and is still the baseline every new retriever is compared against.

## The formula

$$
\text{BM25}(D, Q) = \sum_{t \in Q} \text{idf}(t) \cdot \frac{\text{tf}(t,D)\,(k_1 + 1)}{\text{tf}(t,D) + k_1 \left(1 - b + b \dfrac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
$$

with the commonly used smoothed idf

$$
\text{idf}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{N - \text{df}(t) + 0.5}{\text{df}(t) + 0.5} + 1\right)
$$

Symbols: $\text{tf}(t, D)$ is the count of query term $t$ in document $D$; $|D|$ is the length of $D$ in tokens; $\text{avgdl}$ is the average document length in the collection; $N$ is the number of documents and $\text{df}(t)$ how many contain $t$. Two tunable parameters:

- $k_1$ (typically 1.2 to 2.0) controls **term-frequency saturation**. The TF part tends to $k_1 + 1$ as tf grows, so no term can contribute more than $(k_1+1) \cdot \text{idf}$, however often it repeats.
- $b$ (typically 0.75) controls **length normalisation**, between 0 (ignore length) and 1 (fully normalise). A document twice the average length is treated as if each of its term counts were "worth less".

Write $K = k_1 \left(1 - b + b\,|D|/\text{avgdl}\right)$ for the denominator's length part. The TF factor is then $\text{tf}(k_1+1)/(\text{tf} + K)$.

## Saturation in numbers

With $k_1 = 1.2$ and an average-length document ($K = 1.2$), the TF factor is $2.2\,\text{tf} / (\text{tf} + 1.2)$:

| tf | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TF factor | 1.000 | 1.375 | 1.571 | 1.774 | 1.964 | 2.174 |

The first mention is worth 1.0; going from 1 to 3 mentions adds only 0.57; 100 mentions cannot pass 2.2. Compare raw TF-IDF, where 100 mentions are worth 100 times one.

## Worked example: the same three documents

Use the collection from the previous topic (D1: *reset password account page*, length 4; D2: *password rules password length password expiry*, length 6; D3: *reset router wifi*, length 3), with $k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$ and the query "reset password".

```steps
BM25, step by step
**Collection statistics.** $N = 3$, $\text{avgdl} = (4 + 6 + 3)/3 = 4.333$. Both "reset" and "password" have $\text{df} = 2$, so both get $\text{idf} = \ln\!\left(\frac{3 - 2 + 0.5}{2 + 0.5} + 1\right) = \ln(1.6) = 0.470$.
---
**Length factors** $K = 1.2\,(0.25 + 0.75\,|D|/4.333)$. D1: $1.2\,(0.25 + 0.692) = 1.131$. D2: $1.2\,(0.25 + 1.038) = 1.546$. D3: $1.2\,(0.25 + 0.519) = 0.923$.
---
**D1** (reset 1, password 1): each term gives $0.470 \times \frac{1 \times 2.2}{1 + 1.131} = 0.470 \times 1.0325 = 0.485$. Two terms: $0.971$.
---
**D2** (password 3): $0.470 \times \frac{3 \times 2.2}{3 + 1.546} = 0.470 \times 1.452 = 0.682$. "reset" contributes 0.
---
**D3** (reset 1): $0.470 \times \frac{2.2}{1 + 0.923} = 0.470 \times 1.144 = 0.538$.
---
**Ranking: D1 (0.971) > D2 (0.682) > D3 (0.538).** TF-IDF had D2 first. Saturation capped D2's triple "password" at 1.45 instead of 3, and length normalisation penalised D2 for being the longest document. Matching *both* query terms now wins, which is what a user means.
```

Notice also that D3 (a router page) closed most of its gap to D2: under TF-IDF it scored a third of D2, under BM25 about 79% ($0.538 / 0.682$). It is the shortest document, and one "reset" in a 3-word document is a stronger signal than one in a 30-word document.

```callout-tip
Turning the knobs
$k_1 = 0$ makes the TF factor exactly 1 for any non-zero tf: pure "does the term appear" scoring. Large $k_1$ approaches raw TF. $b = 0$ removes length normalisation, which helps collections where long documents really are more relevant (full articles versus stubs). Defaults ($k_1 \approx 1.2$, $b = 0.75$) are robust; tune them only against a labelled evaluation set.
```

The sim starts on the query "what is E42", scored with BM25: the rare code appears in one document, so its idf dominates and that document ranks first. Now switch the query to "how do I get my money back" or "does it work without wifi": the words the user chose never appear in the right document, so BM25 ranks it low. Keep that failure in mind for the dense retrieval topic. Try $k_1 = 0.3$ and $b = 0$ and see whether the ranking changes.

## In practice

BM25 is cheap, needs no GPU, no training data and no embedding model, works in any language your tokeniser handles, and is excellent for exact identifiers, names and rare terms. Its weakness is **vocabulary mismatch**: "laptop won't turn on" shares no terms with "device fails to power up". In many benchmarks a well-tuned BM25 is surprisingly close to neural retrievers, and on out-of-domain data it sometimes beats them, which is why most production systems keep it alongside dense search (see [Hybrid Search](/learn/retrieval-and-rag/hybrid-and-reranking)).
